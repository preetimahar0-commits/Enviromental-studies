01 — CONTEXT

A fire is easiest to stop in its first few minutes

A wildfire that is caught while it still covers a few square metres can often be contained by a single crew. Left for a few hours, the same ignition can grow into a front that no ground team can approach. The entire value of "early detection" is time: every minute saved between ignition and alert is a minute a fire truck gets to spend driving toward the fire instead of chasing it.

Ground-based lookout towers and public phone reports still catch most fires near towns, but they are blind over forests, grasslands, and remote terrain — which is exactly where satellites are useful: they watch the same stretch of land automatically, day and night, with no observer required.

02 — PRINCIPLE

Fire doesn't need to be seen — it needs to be felt, spectrally

Satellites don't detect flame or smoke the way a human eye would. They carry radiometers that measure radiation across several wavelength bands, and a burning area radiates far more strongly in the mid- and thermal-infrared than the ground around it — often glowing brightly in infrared even under smoke that would completely hide it from a normal camera.

VISIBLE

What the eye would see

Useful for mapping smoke plumes and burn scars, but nearly useless at night and easily blocked by cloud or haze — smoke often hides the fire itself.

MID-IR

Where fires stand out

A flaming fire radiates strongly around 3–5 micrometres. Algorithms flag a pixel as a "hotspot" when it's far brighter here than its surroundings and its history.

THERMAL-IR

Confirming a true fire

Longer-wave infrared (around 10–12 micrometres) helps rule out false alarms like sun glint off water or hot bare rock, which mimic fire in mid-IR alone.

SWIR

Seeing through smoke

Short-wave infrared penetrates smoke better than visible light, letting a satellite confirm a fire's extent even while a plume covers the area.

03 — INSTRUMENTS

The satellites already doing this job

Wildfire monitoring today blends general-purpose Earth-observation satellites, built for weather or land studies, with a new generation of satellites purpose-built for fire alone.

SystemOrbit & revisitWhat it contributes
MODIS (Terra/Aqua) Polar, ~1–2 passes/day NASA's long-running workhorse for the global fire hotspot record, at around 1 km resolution — coarse, but decades of consistent data.
VIIRS (Suomi-NPP / NOAA-20) Polar, ~1–2 passes/day Sharper than MODIS at ~375 m, so it catches smaller and newer fires that MODIS would miss.
GOES-R series Geostationary, continuous Stares at the same hemisphere constantly, trading fine resolution for the ability to watch a fire grow minute by minute.
Sentinel-2 / Landsat Polar, days between passes High spatial detail for mapping burn scars and vegetation dryness, though too infrequent for catching new ignitions alone.
FireSat (Earth Fire Alliance) Planned LEO constellation The first satellites built specifically for fire: six infrared bands, roughly 5 m resolution, aiming for a 20-minute global revisit once the full constellation is in orbit.

05 — BEYOND DETECTION

Prediction: knowing where fire is likely before it starts

Detection tells you a fire has already ignited. Prediction tries to estimate where and when one is likely to ignite and spread, by fusing satellite observations with other data over time.

Fuel dryness

Satellites estimate vegetation moisture and biomass, since dry, dense vegetation burns faster and hotter than green, sparse cover.

Weather fusion

Wind speed, humidity, and temperature from weather satellites and ground stations feed spread models built on fire-behaviour physics.

Terrain & history

Elevation, slope, and a location's past fire frequency all shift risk, since fire climbs slopes faster than it spreads on flat ground.

Machine learning risk maps

Models trained on years of satellite and weather data output a daily risk score per grid cell, used to pre-position crews and equipment.

06 — LIMITS

What still makes this hard

  • 01
    Resolution vs. revisit trade-off

    A satellite that images finely tends to pass over any given spot rarely, and one that watches constantly usually images coarsely. Purpose-built constellations exist precisely to break this trade-off.

  • 02
    Cloud and canopy cover

    Thick cloud can block infrared detection entirely, and dense forest canopy can mask a small ground fire until it grows large enough to breach the treetops.

  • 03
    False positives

    Sun glint off water, hot rooftops, gas flares, and even hot desert sand can mimic a fire's thermal signature, so models need multiple bands to confirm a real fire.

  • 04
    Data volume and latency

    A global constellation generates enormous imagery volumes; processing and alerting fast enough to matter needs efficient onboard or ground-based AI, not just raw compute.

07 — TRY IT YOURSELF

A first-year-friendly mini project

SUGGESTED SCOPE

You don't need a satellite to explore this topic hands-on — free public data and beginner-friendly tools are enough for a solid mini-project.

  1. Pull historical fire hotspot data for a region from NASA's FIRMS (Fire Information for Resource Management System) portal — it's free and exports as CSV.
  2. Plot the hotspots on a map by date using Python (pandas + matplotlib or folium) to visualise how a real fire season progressed.
  3. Add a simple weather dataset (temperature, humidity, wind) for the same region and dates, and look for correlation with fire activity.
  4. Optionally, train a basic classifier (like logistic regression or a small decision tree) to predict high-risk days from the weather features — a gentle first step into the ML side of fire prediction.
